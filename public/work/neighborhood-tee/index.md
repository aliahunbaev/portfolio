I did my 6th grade history project on Jean-Michel Basquiat.

I remember looking through photographs of New York's 1980s art scene and being so fucking jealous. I couldn't believe these brilliant minds all lived in the same city sharing ideas and working together.

And the kids.

The neighborhoods were full of artistic, troublesome kids, graffiti writers, b-boys, poets, rappers, all soaking in the culture around them, unknowingly planting creative seeds for the generation to come.

They did their shit the way birds sing. Expression was ingrained into the fabric of their existence.

![Keith Haring, Grace Jones, Fela Kuti, Basquiat](/images/journal/neighborhood/artscene-haring-basquiat.jpg)

![Spike Lee, Basquiat, Andy Warhol](/images/journal/neighborhood/artscene-spike-warhol.jpg)

![](/images/journal/neighborhood/nyc-1.jpg)

![](/images/journal/neighborhood/nyc-2.jpg)

![](/images/journal/neighborhood/nyc-3.jpg)

Where I grew up, that didn't exist. The people around me didn't hate art, but they found no use in it. We were taught to pursue stability and comfort as the highest ideals.

I got nothing against stacking bread, but I don't think that's an excuse to have a boring life. By high-school, most kids I knew were already working toward a life they didn't want.

Disappointed by my surroundings, I turned inward, living vicariously through books, movies, music.

I dreamt of the day I'd get to leave and start real life.

At 16, my friends and I scraped together enough money for Spirit Airlines tickets and (after being homeless for a night) got a one-room apartment. When I finally stepped into Times Square, it blew my fucking mind.

We climbed the Williamsburg Bridge, visited Aimé Leon Dore, glazed Trevor Gorji at the Fugazi store, enjoyed deep-dish pizza by the skyline, snuck into raves (got acquainted with some girls), played basketball, biked through Central Park, and drew in overpriced cafés.

All the joy and anticipation brewing within me for years came flooding through my soul. Everything I'd seen in books and photos was right in front of me. I felt alive. I felt like I was home.

After that trip, it became painfully clear how much we're missing out on. I'm talking about humans as a species.

So much of our potential for joy and creation goes untapped and wasted every day.

We need to be around people who challenge us, excite us, and help us get to the essence of life. I hope more people get to feel that. I want to create a place where kids feel seen, challenged, and excited to show up. That became the motivation behind Combat.

For our first pop-up in New York, I wanted to pay homage to the beautiful neighborhoods and artists that changed my world back in middle school.

So I came up with the Neighborhood Tee.

![](/images/journal/neighborhood/neighborhood-refs.jpg)

The front features minimal typography — mirroring the earliest graphic tees, which were often used for military recruitment and promotion. It's meant more to represent affiliation than aesthetic beauty. Though I do enjoy the way these simple touches look.

![](/images/journal/neighborhood/neighborhood-front.jpg)

The back features an illustration I drew celebrating the influences that shaped me. A neighborhood united through love and artistry.

![](/images/journal/neighborhood/neighborhood-back.jpg)

![](/images/journal/neighborhood/back-references.jpg)

At the end of the day, a shirt is just a shirt. But I think we can tell powerful stories through what we wear, just as we do through painting, photos, film, writing, and music.

I've always loved clothes (and art) that carry a sense of belonging to something beautiful and strong.

For the first time in my life, as I stand here in Manhattan sharing these drawings and ideas with you — that doesn't feel so far from the truth.
